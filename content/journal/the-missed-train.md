Chapter I — The Missed Train

There is a persistent misconception that lives are redirected by grand decisions. I have found the opposite to be more convincing. They bend because of insignificant failures—because a train is missed, because a 
platform changes, because probability briefly mistakes itself for intention.

It was the 27th of June, last year.

I never boarded the train I was supposed to. Instead, I found myself in another compartment, occupying a seat that was never part of the original itinerary. Across from me sat someone who, at the time, was simply another variable in a carriage full of strangers.
“Someone’s been looking at you.” Sahil’s message arrived before my curiosity did. Ordinarily, I would have dismissed the observation. Certain stories have a reputation for repeating themselves, and I had grown increasingly reluctant to audition for familiar endings. So I ignored it—at least initially.Eventually, I looked. A black suit. Quiet composure. A gaze that neither demanded attention nor entirely surrendered it.
Memory is selective. It forgets conversations, but preserves contrasts.
I remember making an irrational wager with myself. If coincidence had gone through the trouble of arranging this scene, perhaps I should at least acknowledge its existence. I stood, changed seats, and indulged the sort of confidence that only exists before evidence intervenes. The hypothesis was embarrassingly simple: if she was interested, she’d follow.
She didn’t.
In retrospect, probability was merely correcting my optimism. The station approached. Time, as it often does, began collapsing into urgency. There are moments where reason becomes a spectator and instinct assumes authorship. Without rehearsing the consequences, I opened Instagram, typed my username onto the search bar, turned the screen toward her for no more than a few seconds, and left the rest to chance.
No conversation.
No promises.
No expectation.
The experiment concluded before any measurable result appeared.
Three days passed.
The event quietly migrated into the archive of moments that almost happened.
Then, unexpectedly, my phone vibrated.

“Are you the one whom I saw on the train?”

A question capable of resurrecting an entire afternoon.

“Yes,” I replied. “Assalamu Alaikum.”

Looking back, that exchange feels strangely untouched by everything that followed. There was a peculiar comfort in two strangers existing without inherited histories. She knew nothing of the versions of me that had already collapsed under their own weight, and I knew nothing of the chapters she had chosen not to narrate.
For a brief interval, neither of us carried context. Only possibility.
And, although neither of us could have recognized it then, an accidentally missed train had already begun writing a destination neither passenger intended to visit.
